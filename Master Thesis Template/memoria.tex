\begin{abstract}
\thispagestyle{plain}
\setcounter{page}{3}

This thesis examines the phenomenon of cheating in online multiplayer games through a dual approach: a conceptual study and a practical implementation.

The first part offers a theoretical exploration of cheating as a socio-technical and historical process. It begins by reflecting on the inherently competitive nature of human behavior and considers how advances in computing and online infrastructure have enabled new forms of digital confrontation. Cheating is framed not as an isolated anomaly, but as a consequence of structural dynamics within highly competitive virtual ecosystems. The study also traces the evolution of anti-cheat technologies and the emergence of a \textbf{commercially driven underground industry}, emphasizing the limitations of current detection mechanisms and their broader implications for player trust and platform integrity.

The second part adopts an applied perspective. It presents the design and development of a custom cheat for \textbf{Counter-Strike 2}, used as a case study to evaluate the resilience of contemporary anti-cheat systems, with a particular focus on Valve's \textbf{VAC}. This practical component enables the identification of architectural weaknesses, the assessment of real-world detection efficacy, and a reflection on the technical and ethical complexities of securing competitive environments.

The research as a whole articulates cheating in video games as both a \textit{technological} and \textit{cultural} issue—grounded in theory, examined through practice, and situated within a broader discourse on digital integrity. The thesis concludes by outlining strategic recommendations for more adaptive, transparent, and ethically robust anti-cheat frameworks.

\textbf{Keywords:} Cheating, Online Games, Counter-Strike 2, Anti-Cheat Systems, Digital Competition, Game Security, Applied Analysis
\end{abstract}
